Suggested hardware for the transceiver

From Radio Team (Santiago, Andrian, Marcos)To Carlos, Michele, Bikash, GuillemVersion 0.4.4 · Sat 17 Oct 2026
New in v0.4.4 (17 Oct): small additions from the 16-Oct review, no new parts beyond one switch:
  • A TX/RX role switch. The professor picks the sending box after both are loaded, so the role must be set without reflashing; Atenea Q8 asks whether a switch is allowed.
  • The U.FL plug is secured for the drop test.
  • The USB firmware reads sticks formatted by Windows or macOS (FAT32 or exFAT, MBR or GPT). We bring our own sticks.

New in v0.4.3 (16 Oct): low-battery countermeasures. The radio's 3.3 V LDO runs straight from the 3.0–4.2 V cell and that is fine (see Power budget); we added an ideal diode for battery sensing, firmware thresholds, a T0 check at 3.0 V and a buck-boost fallback.

New in v0.4.2 (16 Oct): a professor approved 20 dBm e.i.r.p., so SRI and MRM now run as a frequency-hopping radio at up to 20 dBm (+10.5 dB on the links). No hardware change: same SP4, same omni. The radio's TX bursts move toward the datasheet's 120 mA in every SRI/MRM round, which the radio LDO, the 100 µF at the module and the battery path below already cover. Keep them sized for 120 mA.

New in v0.4 (decided 13 Oct): one Ebyte E01-ML01SP4 per box (nRF24L01+ with PA and LNA, surface-mount, antenna socket on the module) and one flex omni antenna plugged straight into it. No patch boards, no second radio. The SP4 is SMD, so it needs a small breakout from the first bench test.

The radio baseline is 2.4 GHz with the nRF24L01+. Under our split, the electronics team picks the micro and designs the board. This page is the radio team's suggestion, built around every hardware rule in the competition PDF, to save you time. It is not a decision. Please challenge it.

In short

  1. Micro module: Raspberry Pi Pico 2 (RP2350, not the Pico 2 W). It costs about $5, has 4 MB flash to hold the 1 MB file, 520 KB RAM and 3.3 V logic, and it can act as a USB host on any two pins or on its native USB port. It has no radio chip on board, so it is safe under the protocol ban.
  2. Radio: one Ebyte E01-ML01SP4 per box (genuine nRF24L01+ with PA and LNA; SMD 14.85 × 18 mm, 10 pads at 1.27 mm; IPEX antenna socket). VCC is 2.0–3.6 V only, so it gets its own 3.3 V regulator. The same module goes from the bench to the final boxes, because modules shown at Quick Mode are final; that means a small breakout for the bench. The radio team's driver is written and tested. (v0.3 had two DP5 modules with patches; v0.2 the Adafruit RFM69HCW at 868 MHz.)
  3. Board: a custom 2-layer carrier PCB that the Pico 2 and the radio solder onto. It also carries the radio regulator, the USB-A host port, the battery charger, the 5 V supply for the stick, ESD protection, and the switch, button and LEDs. The antenna is a Molex flex omni stuck on a box wall, its cable plugged into the radio module: no RF circuitry on our board. Before Quick Mode we use a perfboard prototype with the same modules.
  4. Power: one protected 18650 Li-ion cell, charged on board through a charge-only USB-C port. Our whole competition day uses roughly 10–20 % of it.
  5. Estimated cost: about €40–55 per device with a lab-made PCB, or about €30–40 with an external fab, against the €150 cap.

Battery sizing

A sending round averages about 150 mA. Idle with the radio listening is about 70 mA. A full competition day of ~4 hours powered on needs about 300–600 mAh.

One 2500 mAh 18650 covers that about four times over. A smaller LiPo would also do, but pouch cells are more fragile in the drop test.

Low battery: the LDO is fine

The LDO gives a clean 3.3 V down to a cell of about 3.4 V (dropout ≤ 0.1 V at 150 mA). Below that it passes the cell through: about 2.95 V at the 3.0 V cutoff, still inside the SP4's 2.0–3.6 V. The cell spends ~90 % of its charge above 3.4 V. An LDO after the 5 V boost (~60 % efficient, boost ripple at the radio) or a 5 V battery would be worse.

  • Battery sense: an ideal diode (e.g. LM66100) instead of the Schottky into VSYS, so ADC3 reads the cell.
  • Firmware: ≥ 3.5 V normal; 3.4–3.5 V warning LED; below 3.4 V at setup a loud warning (never block a round); below 3.2 V write received data to flash after every burst.
  • T0 measures the radio output at 3.6, 3.3 and 3.0 V.
  • Fallback: a buck-boost (TPS63001/TPS63020) if T0 shows more than 2 dB less output at 3.0 V; it needs a filter at the module.

For the sustainability report

We can measure watts per round directly on this design. The parts are rechargeable and RoHS. The modules unsolder and can be reused, and the board shape lets you repair rather than replace.

Risks and how we cover them

RiskMitigation
PIO-USB host fails with some USB sticksTest 3–4 different sticks in stage 1 (FAT32 and exFAT, formatted on Windows and on a Mac; the firmware reads both and MBR or GPT layouts, host-tested). We bring our own sticks. The RP2350's native USB can also be a host, so a second path exists on the same chip.
The foil-wrapped setup makes the USB port hard to reachPut the port on a side wall. Ask on Atenea whether the foil may be opened at the port during the 5-min setup.
Brown-out or noise during radio TXSeparate radio LDO and bulk capacitance at each module; battery sense on ADC3 with firmware thresholds; check the radio at 3.0 V in T0 and the box at low charge in stage 3.
Clone radio chips (Si24R1)Buy only the plain E01-ML01SP4 part number from Ebyte's store or a distributor; T0/T2 check the chip.
SMD module needs a breakout before any PCB existsA one-evening lab board (or careful wiring) in stage 1; the carrier PCB takes over in stage 3.
Drop test breaks a joint or ejects the batteryNo sockets; standoffs; strapped cell; drop rev A on purpose in week 9.
The "attacks" interrupt the link mid-roundWatchdog and brown-out reset; firmware resumes from the last acknowledged line (protocols team).
